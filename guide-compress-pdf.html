How to Compress a PDF for Email (and When Not To)

You finished the document, attached it to an email — and the email bounced back: file too large. Most email services cap attachments at around 25 MB, and some work or university inboxes are stricter still. Compressing the PDF is usually the fastest fix, but it does not work on every file. This guide explains when it helps, how to do it in under a minute, and what to try when it does not.

Compress your PDF in 3 steps

  1. Open the Compress PDF tool and upload your PDF (up to 50 MB). The compression runs entirely in your browser — a private document is never uploaded to a server.
  2. Compress and check the result. TrustFile tells you honestly how much smaller the file got. If a file barely shrinks — or even grows slightly — it says so plainly, and you can still download the original instead of a worse copy.
  3. Open the compressed file once to confirm the pages still look right, then attach it to your email.

When compression works well

Compression shrinks a PDF mainly by squeezing the images inside it. So it works best on the files that are usually too big for email in the first place:

  • Scanned documents and photo-heavy PDFs — a scanned form, a photo catalogue, or slides saved as PDF. These often shrink a lot.
  • Reports with large embedded images — charts, screenshots and photos at print quality take far more space than the text around them.
  • Files saved at print resolution — a document made for a printer carries far more image detail than a screen or an email ever needs.

When compression will not help much

Some PDFs are already as small as they can get, and no honest tool will pretend otherwise:

  • Text-only PDFs — a few pages of plain text are already tiny. There is almost nothing left to squeeze out.
  • Already-compressed files — a PDF that was already compressed once, or built from already-compressed JPG images, has little left to save.
  • Very small files — if your PDF is under a megabyte, the attachment limit is probably not the problem; check the email size limit on the receiving side instead.

If TrustFile reports little or no saving, believe it: re-compressing the same file again will not magically shrink it further, and repeated compression only risks degrading the images.

What to do instead, when compression is not enough

  • Split the PDF: if only part of the document is needed now, use the Split PDF tool to send just those pages, and send the rest separately.
  • Remove unneeded pages: delete blank pages, covers or appendices with the Delete Pages tool before compressing.
  • Send a link instead of an attachment: upload the file to a cloud drive and email the link — best for very large files, though it means the file does leave your device, unlike a TrustFile conversion.
  • Ask what the recipient actually needs: a university office or client may only need a few pages, not the whole 80-page report.

A note on quality

Compressing a PDF is a trade-off: smaller file, slightly lower image quality. For emailing a form, an assignment or a CV, that trade-off is almost always worth it — the text stays sharp, and nobody prints an email attachment at poster size. But keep your original file: never compress the only copy of a document you may need at full quality later. Compress a copy, send the copy, keep the original.

Frequently asked questions

Is compressing a PDF really free?
Yes. There is no signup, no daily limit and no watermark. The compression runs in your browser.

Will my file be uploaded anywhere?
No. The whole process happens on your own device. When you close the page, nothing remains.

Why did my PDF barely shrink?
Most likely it is mostly text, or its images were already compressed. Try splitting out only the pages you need, or send a cloud-drive link for very large files.
